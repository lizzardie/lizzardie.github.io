Project Overview

The purpose of this project was to investigate the relationship between hidden cell towers and wealthy areas. My original hypothesis was that wealthy areas would have a higher percentage of hidden cell towers compared to their less wealthy counterparts because wealth usually entitles people to a level of physical and visual distance from reality because you can afford to have those kinds of preferences made into reality. In this case, the payment is with time, but that is another thing that money buys you back from capitalism. In the process, I encountered the legal framework of cell tower construction, and discovered that the hidden cell towers didn't stem solely from wealthy individual or community pressures, but from the federal government's restriction of state and local governments' ability to meaningfully regulate cell tower construction through other means.

Why Should I Care?

The subject of this investigation probably doesn't stir many people to any strong emotion either way. There's a large demand for better and better quality internet service from all providers, and companies do their best to meet this demand. The circumstances of cell tower construction might seem to many an incidental and necessary part of building critical infrastructure.

However, the circumstances illuminated below are far from necessary and inevitable. They reflect a larger pattern of nonpartisan government neglect and corporate greed.

As early as 1979, we had quantitative knowledge of the effects of communication towers on bird mortality rates [1]. Banks' report compiled the mortality rate of birds from all causes, direct and indirect, and used even earlier reports of cell towers leading to dead birds. Three separate multiple year long studies of annual bird mortality at single towers all came out to about 2,600 dead birds, most of these deaths at night. Meaning that 2,600 dead birds per year per tower is a reasonable consideration. Banks' goes on that without a statistical analysis of what factors of the towers specifically led to these rates, it is unclear how many such towers should be assumed to cause this level of mortality rate. However, as of 1979, with only 1,000 TV transmission stations in the country he says that even if only half of all towers were so positioned, that would still mean an annual mortality rate of 1.25 million birds.

At the time of an American Bird Conservancy(ABC) report in 2000, with the number of communication towers sitting at around 77,000, and an estimated 5,000 new towers being built every year, a compilation of studies on lit towers over 199ft above ground level (no data on height above sea level) found that 230 species of birds were being killed, with 52 of those species being in need of active population management and protection [2]. Within just the limited studies that this report gathered, half a million birds were killed at several sites with less than a year of observation. Alongside the greater annual averages of these deaths, there were also several incidents of thousands of birds dying in single nights in the case of weather like low clouds or fog. While more active research had begun into the exact cause of these deaths, and it was speculated that the lights and height were the main factors, no conclusion had been reached.

The FCC ignored both the ABC's report and recommendations, and the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service's(FWS) guidelines for minimizing bird deaths for years. It took a court order in 2008 for them to be found legally responsible for regulating based on the National Environmental Policy Act(NEPA)'s and the Endangered Species Act's(ESA) standards [3].

The most current recommendations (2021) from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service state that new communications towers should be grouped with existing towers or infrastructure and that whenever possible, all towers should be shorter than 199ft above ground level. It also recommends the nighttime lighting system use flashing red lights instead of steadily burning ones, and that existing tower owners with non-flashing light towers should submit plans to transfer their lighting system[4].

The non-flashing lights confuse the nocturnally migrating birds and either send them crashing into the towers or draw their attention long enough to tire them out and get them killed farther on in their journey. In 2015, the FCC decided at last to require that new towers higher than 150ft above ground level use flashing lights, but refused "to mandate that the tens of thousands of existing towers be retrofitted"[5]. This is still their position, in direct defiance of current recommendations, with birds still dying from cell towers at an estimated rate of about 7 million annually. This 7 million estimate is itself a reduction from the estimates that arose in the decades between the original Banks' report and the 2015 requirement change.

Another broader consideration is that laws like the ones discussed below are essentially a punishment for the slowness of government beauocracy to the benefit of not-the-government. And the FCC's flagrant refusal to engage meaninfully with the job of environmental regulation that only they are allowed to do is an intentional and effective play for money. And it proves, as these things tend to, that if they wanted to be efficient for your benefit, they could. The corporate bribery runs deep and it extends to every aspect of law in this country. The kind of for-sale policy that emerges for regulating the environmental harm of cell towers is the same kind of policy that emerges for prison law, that emerges for healthcare law, that emerges for privacy law.

The Telecommunications Act of 1996 and Related Legislation

The Telecommunications Act of 1996:

  • ammended The Communications Act of 1934, the original Act which created the FCC
  • limited the regulatory power of state and local governments
  • promoted the deployment of wireless services by seeking to minimize construction delays

Section 704[6]: Section 332(c) (47 U.S.C. 332(c)) is amended by adding limitations to local authority. One section of this ammendment reads as follows:

  • "(ii) A State or local government or instrumentality thereof shall act on any request for authorization to place, construct, or modify personal wireless service facilities (aka aka cell tower, aka communications tower, additional context mine) within a reasonable period of time after the request is duly filed with such government or instrumentality, taking into account the nature and scope of such request."

The actual amount of time of the phrase "reasonable period of time", and what a local government's "failure to act" within this time frame means, are not specified until an FCC ruling in 2009. This ruling declared a "reasonable period of time" to be 90 days for additions to existing cell tower sites (a modification or collocation project) or 150 days for new sites. Another ammendment to the "failure to act" clause came in 2015, which reduced this "reasonable period of time" to only 60 days for construction that did not count as "substantial change"[7].

  • "(C)(2) Timeframe for review. Within 60 days of the date on which an applicant submits a request seeking approval under this section, the State or local government shall approve the application unless it determines that the application is not covered by this section.
  • "(C)(3) Tolling of the timeframe for review. The 60-day period begins to run when the application is filed, and may be tolled only by mutual agreement or in cases where the reviewing State or local government determines that the application is incomplete. The timeframe for review is not tolled by a moratorium on the review of applications.
  • "(C)(4) Failure to act. In the event the reviewing State or local government fails to approve or deny a request seeking approval under this section within the timeframe for review (accounting for any tolling), the request shall be deemed granted. The deemed grant does not become effective until the applicant notifies the applicable reviewing authority in writing after the review period has expired (accounting for any tolling) that the application has been deemed granted."

§ 1.6100 of the 2015 ruling mentioned and quoted above also includes in (C)(4) that a "failure to act", or a lapse in response for 60 days, means that construction is automatically approved by the company sending in a letter saying that it is (which sounds like a joke but it is not).

Policy Outcomes and Public Attitudes

As a result of the difficulty that state and local governments faced in attempting to regulate the construction of cell towers, they turned to regulating the sight of them. Of course, there had already been complaints about the sight of cell tower infrastructure before the Telecommunications Act of 1996, as evidenced by the fact that companies Raymont and Larson Camouflage (still two of the three biggest names in the industry) had already created several disguised cell towers[8]. But in 1996, regulating the optics became a subject of much more importance to small and local governments who could regulate cell tower placement by enacting new ordinances which allowed them to make companies "submit visual impact statements, consider alternate sites, and disguise the towers by concealment, colocation, or camoflauge"[9].

The 2021 Orange County Public Works Cell Tower Proposal Submission Checklist, which covers any cell towers constructed along County roads, is made up of 3 elements[10]. The third is the "wireless facility design standards" element, which contains about half of the checklist items. Among them are things like:

  • "A new tower visible by a motorist driving on a scenic highway (including landscape corridors and viewscape corridors as defined in the Orange County General Plan) shall be stealth and placed or constructed so that the entire wireless facility is below any major ridgeline (as defined by the General Plan or Specific Plan)."

and (referencing the first example):

  • "If the applicant demonstrates to the satisfaction of the approving authority that stealthing the facility is infeasible then the applicant shall be required to camouflage the facility."

and:

  • "Wireless communications facilities are prohibited in all center street medians."

California Assembly Bill 57, which passed in 2015, mandates a period for public comment on cell tower construction proposals in unincorporated neighborhoods[11]. This was predictably favored by conservationists and residents of unincorporated areas who didn't like the way cell towers marred the visual landscape, and was overwhelmingly contested by companies[12].

As a matter of self-interest, companies have predictably not backed down from their attempts to minimize friction at either end of their interaction with the government, whether that be federal, state, or local. In 2023, the National Telecommunications and Information Administration(NTIA) created the National Environment Policy Act(NEPA) Procedures and Categorical Exclusions document in order to remove the need for environmental reveiw on actions that similar departments also did not require review for, like administrative tasks and testing that would reveal if an environmental review was needed. The public comments, all from wireless companies and related infrastructure associations, included such absurd requests as allowing companies to use "segmentation" in order to get around the EPA's requirements[13].

Project Limitations and Next Steps

In its current iteration, this project is more of a literature review and the beginning stages of a long term research/data collection project. There is no responsible way for me to make any claims with the current data pool, but with extended documentation, a pattern might emerge. I'm also interested in if there's any merit to the idea of quantifying the quality of hiding that these hidden towers display, because some are extremely convincing and others are extremely obvious, and I feel like that should factor into the conversation of wealth and the optics associated with it.

  • Make a FOIA request to the FCC.
    • I would like to look at records of cell tower build and site requests that have been submitted in Orange County, and especially ones that were not approved and why.
  • Continue to collect hidden cell tower data in my day to day.
    • Now that I am actively looking out for them, I'm hopeful that I will see plenty more hidden cell towers by just tracing my usual steps with a keener eye. Additionally, I plan on doing several more active searches, especially in areas like Irvine which have a pretty strict standard of concealment. I anticipate this part of the project generally being an on-going background activity as I slowly build out these data points.
  • Learn how to use code to scrape data.
    • I would love to be able to scrape the US Census Data and the FOIA request results for exactly what I'm looking for. It would also give me the freedom to not have to rely on the data summaries that other sources do of data like that from the US Census.
  • Examine alternate ways of considering cell tower distribution.
    • Considering County-wide laws around cell tower placement along highways, I wonder if there might be a higher concentration of hidden towers within a certain distance from highways. Additionally, one of the three lead companies for these projects, Raycap, formerly Larson, worked with Disneyland before they got into the hidden cell tower market, and that brings up the question of density vs highest number.
